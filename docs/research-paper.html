Label-Free Validation of an Agentic Multi-LLM Question-Answering System

Swaroop Naik
Department of Information Technology, Vidyalankar Institute of Technology, Mumbai, India
naikswaroop1910@gmail.com · github.com/Swaroop192005/V2_Agentic_LLM_System
Abstract —

Large language models (LLMs) answer open-ended questions fluently regardless of whether the answer is correct, and cannot reliably self-assess. We present an agentic multi-LLM system that decides how far to trust its own output without any ground-truth answer key. Two models (LLaMA 3, Mistral) generate candidate answers independently; two further models (phi3 as Verifier, qwen3.5:9b as Judge) score both candidates blindly on a shared eight-factor rubric; three external checks — cross-model semantic agreement, and relevance-gated lookups against Wikipedia and Wikidata — corroborate the result; and a six-signal weighted confidence score gates a regeneration loop. Because no correctness labels exist in the target domain, every design decision is justified by label-free objectives — discrimination tests, internal consistency, and regeneration validity — rather than accuracy against a target. We report a complete 5,000-question dataset (8,432 logged attempts) generated under one consistent, bias-controlled regime, and four principal findings: (i) a judge model must be selected on per-criterion score variance, not mean or ceiling behaviour — a candidate with competitive averages scored all eight criteria identically on 57.5% of items; (ii) a naive evaluator design silently coupled the two judges (32 of 37 total scores identical) until made blind; (iii) answer-position and model-identity bias inflated an apparent quality gap that halved once evaluation was randomized and blinded; and (iv) the regeneration threshold, calibrated via a purpose-built control group and confirmed by a two-proportion test (p=0.011), ROC/Youden's J analysis, and bootstrap (95% CI [0.69, 0.75]), settles at 0.75. We further document three measurement errors detected and corrected before they became conclusions, and argue that this self-correction record is itself central to the credibility of label-free evaluation.

Index Terms — LLM-as-judge, multi-agent systems, answer validation, evaluation without ground truth, position bias, confidence calibration, retrieval-augmented generation.

I.Introduction

A language model asked a difficult question returns a coherent, confident answer whether or not it is factually correct, and its own reported certainty is a poor guide to correctness. For any system that must act on model output, the operative question is therefore not "what is the answer?" but "how much should this answer be trusted, and what should be done when trust is low?"

The intuitive remedy — asking a second model to check the first — fails quietly. A checker model drawn from the same family shares the generator's blind spots and, as an evaluator, exhibits a strong tendency to endorse fluent text.[1],[2] Worse, in many practical domains there is no labelled correctness signal to audit the checker against, so the usual escape hatch — measure accuracy — is unavailable.

This work confronts that constraint directly. We build an agentic pipeline in which trust is established through independent corroboration — multiple models that cross-examine one another, plus external knowledge sources — and is expressed as a single calibrated confidence score that gates regeneration. The central contribution is not the pipeline itself but a demonstrated, repeatable method for validating and defending such a system where no ground truth exists, together with a running, auditable record of the measurement errors caught along the way.

Concretely, we contribute: (1) a six-stage bias-controlled architecture with blind, order-randomized dual evaluation; (2) a family of label-free validation objectives that made each model and parameter choice falsifiable; (3) a control-group solution to the censored-data problem inherent in calibrating a regeneration threshold; and (4) a fully instrumented 5,000-question dataset storing every raw signal, released with the source.

II.Background and Related Work

The "LLM-as-a-judge" paradigm uses a strong model to score or rank the outputs of others, and correlates reasonably with human preference on aggregate benchmarks.[1] Its known pathologies motivate much of our design. LLM judges exhibit position bias (favouring an answer by its slot), verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias;[2] purpose-built evaluator models such as Prometheus attempt to mitigate these with rubric fine-tuning[7] but impose their own output formats.

Our corroboration signals draw on established components: BERTScore for semantic similarity between generated texts,[3] Sentence-BERT embeddings for question–answer and query–document relevance,[4] and retrieval-augmented generation for grounding answers in external documents.[5] The idea that agreement among independently sampled reasoning paths signals reliability underlies self-consistency decoding.[6] For calibration without labels we borrow from diagnostic-test statistics: Youden's J for optimal-cutoff selection[8] and the Wilson score interval for small-sample proportions.[9] Unlike benchmark studies that assume a reference answer, our setting has none; our methodological focus is precisely the validation techniques that remain available under that constraint.

III.System Architecture

The pipeline (Fig. 1) runs six stages per question. Stages 1–2 generate two candidate answers independently with LLaMA 3 and Mistral (sampling temperature 0.7). Stage 3 (Verifier, phi3) and Stage 5 (Judge, qwen3.5:9b) each score both candidates on the same eight-factor rubric — Factual Correctness, Question Relevance, Context Relevance, Faithfulness, Completeness, Hallucination-Free, Clarity, and Consistency, each 0–10 for a total of 80 — at temperature 0.1 for determinism; only the Judge additionally declares a winner. Stage 4 computes semantic and external signals in parallel. Stage 6 (Combiner, LLaMA 3, temperature 0.4) synthesises the shipped answer from the best attempt.

A. Confidence signal and gate

Six signals are combined into a single 0–1 confidence score for the winning answer (Table I). Any signal unavailable for a question — most often an external source with no relevant match — has its weight redistributed proportionally over the rest, so the score remains well-defined rather than penalising the answer for a missing lookup. If the score falls below the threshold τ=0.75, both answers are regenerated from scratch, up to two retries (three attempts total), after which the highest-scoring attempt is shipped.

B. External corroboration sources

Both fact-checks query the live public APIs of the official sites, with no API key. Wikipedia uses the English MediaWiki search API (en.wikipedia.org/w/api.php) to locate the most relevant article and the REST summary endpoint for its lead paragraph; the answer is scored by cosine similarity to that summary. Wikidata uses entity label search (wbsearchentities) over proper-noun candidates extracted from the answer, scoring against the matched entity's structured description. Critically, both apply a relevance gate: if the question–source similarity is below 0.35, the match is discarded and the signal abstains rather than injecting an unrelated source as evidence. This is why external coverage is deliberately partial (Wikipedia 53.9%, Wikidata 40.6% of questions) — many compound analytical questions have no dedicated encyclopedic entry, and a forced match would add noise, not signal.

IV.Methodology: Validation Without Labels

With no correctness labels, "better" cannot mean "more accurate against a target." We instead adopt two families of label-free objective, each of which can fail — the property that makes them genuine tests rather than assumptions.

Discrimination. A usable evaluator must score a deliberately broken answer far below a solid one on the same question. Constructed adversarial pairs give a direct, label-free floor on evaluator quality.

Internal consistency and regeneration validity. Signals that measure a shared latent "quality" should predict one another; a weighting under which they do not is capturing noise. And a meaningful confidence threshold should trigger regeneration mainly when a second attempt genuinely improves the answer on independent signals (the judge excluded, to avoid circularity).

Two bias controls are built into data generation. First, the physical assignment of each model to the "Answer 1"/"Answer 2" slot is randomized per question by a coin flip, and all brand identifiers are stripped from evaluator prompts, so neither position nor identity can leak into a score; the true model behind each slot is recorded separately for later attribution. Second, whenever the judge model or scoring standard changed during development, the dataset was restarted from scratch — mixing two labelling regimes teaches any downstream analysis to average two disagreeing standards, an error made once early and never repeated.

V.Experimental Setup

All models run locally through Ollama on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB). Because four distinct 6–9 GB models must be resident across a question, concurrency is fixed at one to avoid VRAM over-subscription; this also improved per-question latency by removing contention. The question bank comprises open-ended, compound technical prompts (e.g. "compare and contrast…", "walk through the failure modes of…") across science, engineering, finance, and computing. The final corpus contains 5,000 questions and 8,432 logged attempts; every attempt stores all raw per-criterion scores and signal values, so any weighting or threshold can be recomputed post hoc without regenerating data. Retrieval uses the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence encoder with a 0.35 relevance floor.

VI.Results

A. Judge selection by per-criterion variance

Three judge models were compared on 40 blind evaluations each. Mean score alone was misleading: the strongest-averaging open candidate, CompassJudger-1, assigned all eight rubric criteria an identical value on 57.5% of items — fluent boilerplate around a single number, contributing no per-criterion information. qwen3.5:9b never produced a flat score (Table II). Selection on this "flat-rate" diagnostic, invisible to totals-only comparison, was decisive.

B. A hidden coupling between the two evaluators

An audit found the Judge's eight scores identical to the Verifier's, digit-for-digit, on 32 of 37 questions. The Judge's prompt had been supplied the Verifier's numeric scores as "context," and at low temperature it reproduced them rather than forming an independent view — collapsing the "second opinion" signal into a copy. Removing the Verifier's output from the Judge's prompt restored genuine independence, after which Verifier–Judge agreement became a real, varying signal.

C. Position and identity bias

Before the bias controls, one model was always shown first and prompts named the brands, confounding position and identity with quality throughout the early data. Measured on the final bias-controlled corpus, win-rate by raw slot is strongly skewed, but win-rate by true model identity is far closer to even (Table III) — direct evidence that most of the apparent gap was an artefact of ordering, with a residual tendency to favour whichever answer is read second sitting atop a modest genuine difference.

D. Threshold calibration under censored data

Calibrating the regeneration threshold is complicated by censoring: a second attempt is only ever generated below the threshold, so the data above it is unobserved. We introduced a control group — a random fraction of already-passing answers receive a research-only retry — supplying unbiased retry-improvement data across the full score range. Three independent methods concur (Table IV): a two-proportion z-test on adjacent score bands, an ROC/Youden's J optimal-cutoff analysis with inverse-probability sampling correction, and a 2,000-sample bootstrap. The threshold 0.75 lies inside the bootstrap 95% interval on a broad near-optimal plateau.

Under τ=0.75, the shipped answers have a mean confidence of 0.734 (SD 0.044; range 0.451–0.856). Notably, 63.3% of final answers ship below τ: with a fixed three-attempt cap, "threshold" means "try up to three times to reach 0.75, then ship the best," not a hard guarantee — a property we surface explicitly rather than obscure.

E. Is the model-identity gap real? Verifier discrimination

The Judge rates Mistral higher specifically on factual criteria, yet the independent Verifier shows no gap between the two generators on any criterion. To distinguish "no real difference" from "insensitive instrument," we applied the discrimination test to the Verifier: on constructed pairs it scored solid answers 77–80/80 against deliberately broken ones at 0–8/80 (mean gap 74.5). The Verifier is therefore demonstrably capable of detecting real quality gaps, which shifts the balance of evidence toward the residual Mistral preference being a judge-specific perception rather than a verified quality advantage. We report this as an open question, not a settled claim.

VII.Discussion

A recurring theme is that aggregate statistics conceal the failures that matter: a competitive mean hid a non-discriminating judge; similar Verifier–Judge scores hid a copy, not a consensus; a plausible win-rate hid a position artefact. Each was exposed only by looking one level below the summary — at per-criterion variance, at digit-level identity, at position-versus-identity decomposition. For evaluation systems without ground truth, we argue this "look below the aggregate" discipline is not optional polish but the primary source of validity.

Equally, three analysis errors were caught and corrected before being reported: a model's self-reported rubric total that disagreed with its own criterion scores (now always recomputed); an ostensibly independent "second-opinion" signal that was mathematically derived from the judge and inflated a consistency metric (excluded from the search); and a threshold analysis that under-counted control samples after a mid-run change in sampling rate (re-weighted by the rate actually in force). Each surfaced because a result looked "too clean." We contend that a transparent self-correction record is the strongest available evidence of trustworthiness precisely when external ground truth is absent.

VIII.Limitations

The system measures self-consistency and external corroboration, not verified correctness; no result is validated against a human answer key. The Mistral-versus-LLaMA 3 question remains open pending per-answer human fact-checking, for which a blind, balanced rating harness is released but not yet completed. The Verifier (phi3) was never swapped and clusters near the score ceiling on ordinary answers, so it has not received the model-comparison scrutiny the Judge did. The relevance and retrieval gates (all 0.35) were validated only as "not obviously broken" by a blind spot-check, not proven optimal. Finally, all findings are specific to this model set, rubric, and question bank; transfer to other settings is expected in method but not in tuned values, and is untested.

IX.Conclusion and Future Work

We presented an agentic multi-LLM system that establishes trust in its own answers through blind, bias-controlled, independently corroborated evaluation, and a label-free methodology for validating every part of it without a ground-truth answer key. The contribution is a reusable protocol — discrimination testing, bias decomposition, and control-group calibration — evidenced at scale on a released 5,000-question dataset, and a candid account of the measurement errors that discipline caught. Future work is a small human-labelled study to convert the system's self-consistent claims into verified ones, subjecting the Verifier to the same scrutiny as the Judge, and calibrating the retrieval gate against logged real-world usage now that instrumentation is in place.
